Oxygen concentrators in India: technology, market, import licensing, manufacturing

Step 2 of the Jiayi / Jeauyy study · prepared 13 Nov 2026 · based on public sources listed at the end. This is research, not legal advice. Before you commit money, confirm licensing and duty points with a CDSCO regulatory consultant and a customs broker (CHA). Rules change often.

Summary

  • What they are: machines that pull nitrogen out of room air with a mineral filter (zeolite) and deliver 90–96% oxygen. The method, pressure-swing adsorption (PSA), dates from 1960 and is not patented. What is still protected are specific newer designs (Inogen, Philips and others).
  • Who buys in India: (1) long-term oxygen patients: COPD (around 55 million Indians), post-TB lung damage and lung fibrosis; (2) high-altitude travel: Ladakh, Spiti, Sikkim, Char Dham/Kedarnath, Amarnath, Kailash; (3) institutions working at altitude: Army, ITBP, BRO, tunnel and hydro projects, mines; (4) home-care and rental companies.
  • Competition: imported portables sell for ₹1.25–2.1 lakh (Oxymed P2 ₹1,24,500; Inogen Rove 6 ₹1,93,999; Philips SimplyGo Mini ₹2,10,700). 5 L home units sell for ₹32k–58k. A Jiayi-class portable landed at roughly ₹40–45k could retail at ₹65–90k, which is well under the brands.
  • To import legally you need: a company, GST and IEC; a medical-device sale registration (MD-42) from your state; and a CDSCO import licence (Form MD-15) for every model. An oxygen concentrator is a Class B device. Also needed: Legal Metrology importer registration, e-waste and battery EPR registration, and correct labelling and MRP. Plan on 4–8 months to the first legal sale.
  • Biggest constraint with Jiayi: only models registered in China (JY-P25B, JY-P37A, JY-P05A, JY-Y05B) can get the Chinese export or free-sale certificate that the MD-15 needs. The M1 and S1-A are "health-care" units with no registration, so they can't be licensed in India as they stand.
  • The quickest legal shortcut: work through a firm that already holds Indian licences and can act as authorised agent/importer, so you only need to sell under MD-42. Selling these as "wellness" devices or importing them as "personal use" is not a workaround. Customs has been chasing exactly this.
  • Manufacturing in India is possible: Class B manufacturing licence MD-5 from the state, after a Notified Body audit. It is a real engineering company, though. Expect 2–3 years, a 10–15 person team, and roughly ₹6–15 crore. The compressor and the zeolite will still be imported at first. Patent risk can be managed with an Indian freedom-to-operate search and your own design. Jiayi's Chinese patents don't apply in India unless they filed here.

1. How these machines work

  1. Compress: an oil-free compressor pushes filtered room air into one of two columns ("sieve beds") filled with zeolite beads (lithium-exchanged LiX or 13X molecular sieve).
  2. Adsorb: under pressure the zeolite traps nitrogen and lets oxygen (and argon) through. The oxygen collects in a small tank.
  3. Swing: after a few seconds, solenoid valves switch the air to the second bed. The first bed is vented to room pressure, which releases its nitrogen, and a little oxygen is used to purge it clean. This repeats continuously, which is why it is called pressure-swing adsorption.
  4. Measure and deliver: an ultrasonic O₂ sensor checks purity (alarms below about 82–85%). A stationary unit sends a steady flow (1–5 or 1–10 L/min) through a humidifier bottle. A portable unit uses pulse dosing: a pressure sensor detects the start of each breath and fires a short burst (bolus) of oxygen, usually 10–90 ml. Air breathed out wastes nothing, so a much smaller compressor, smaller beds and a battery are enough.
Why purity tops out at about 95%: argon (0.93% of air) passes through with the oxygen, so the practical ceiling is around 95.5%. "93% ± 3%" is the normal medical spec, and ISO 80601-2-69 is the international standard for these machines.
Why "5 L" on a portable is misleading: Jiayi's own manual gives 210–1,050 ml of O₂ per minute at settings 1–5. Inogen, Philips and Oxymed portables are in the same ~1 L/min class (for example the Inogen Rove 4 delivers up to 840 ml/min). The "litre-equivalent" wording is industry marketing. Only stationary units give true litres per minute.
Altitude: thinner air means less air per compressor stroke. Jiayi's registered spec allows operation down to 56 kPa (about 4,700 m), but its adverts claim 6,000 m. Don't repeat the 6,000 m claim in India without test data.

Duty rates come from Indian tariff databases and the 2020 Health Cess notification. Confirm the current rates with your broker for each shipment. Under a 70% trade-margin cap the maximum MRP is 1.7 × your price to distributor, e.g. ₹50k to distributor → MRP up to ₹85k. NPPA's last extension notice for that cap is dated 29 Mar 2023; check whether it still applies. Separately, a medical device's MRP can't rise more than 10% in 12 months.

At ₹65–90k MRP a licensed, warrantied portable undercuts Oxymed P2 (₹1.25L) and the global brands (₹1.9–2.1L) while keeping a healthy margin. Price is not the problem; after-sales service and trust are. Budget for spare units, a repair bench and a 2–3 year warranty.

Documents you must get from Jiayi for the MD-15

  • Power of Attorney appointing your company as Indian authorised agent, apostilled. China joined the Apostille Convention in Nov 2023.
  • Free Sale Certificate / export sales certificate from Chinese regulators. China's new export-certificate rules took effect on 1 May 2026. This is only possible for registered products: P25B (川械注准20242080027), P37A (20242080417), P05A (20222080246) and Y05B (20222080135).
  • ISO 13485 certificate for the factory. Ask for the certificate and the certification body; I couldn't verify one. A CE certificate would help but isn't mandatory for Class B.
  • Plant Master File and Device Master File in CDSCO format. The DMF covers design, materials, risk management (ISO 14971), software validation, stability and shelf life, sterilisation (none here) and labelling.
  • Test reports: IEC 60601-1 (electrical safety), IEC 60601-1-2 (EMC), IEC 60601-1-11 (home use), ISO 80601-2-69 (concentrator), ISO 80601-2-67 (pulse/conserving function), IEC 62133 / UN 38.3 (battery), ISO 10993 for the cannula.
  • Indian labels and IFU in English: importer name, address and licence number, MRP, manufacturing date, batch/serial, "Rx" where applicable.
The M1 and S1-A problem: they're sold in China as "health-care" units under a company standard and have no medical registration, so Jiayi can't issue a free-sale certificate for them. They also can't be imported into India as non-medical goods: "oxygen concentrator" is on CDSCO's Class B list whatever the marketing says. The options are: (a) Jiayi registers them in China; (b) Jiayi gets CE/MDR or another approval first; or (c) you start with P25B, P37A or P05A, which overlap in use.

Suggested sequence

  1. Month 0–1: company, GST, IEC; hire an RA consultant; ask Jiayi for the document pack above. Check whether Jiayi has already appointed an Indian agent: their UK reseller suggests they export.
  2. Month 1–2: MD-42 for your warehouse; MD-17 test licence for 3–5 samples; field-test samples in Leh or Manali and with 2–3 pulmonologists.
  3. Month 2–6: file MD-14 (one manufacturing site + 1–4 products); EPR, LMPC and BIS checks in parallel.
  4. Month 5–8: MD-15 granted → first commercial shipment → launch through rental partners and clinics.

6.3 Patents: what can bite, and how to design around it

  • Patents are territorial. Only patents granted in India can stop you making, importing or selling here. Indian patents last 20 years; India has no utility models; registered designs last 10 + 5 years.
  • Free to use: basic two-bed PSA (Skarstrom cycle, 1960s), standard zeolites, basic pulse/demand oxygen conserving (1980s–90s), flowmeters and humidifiers. All expired.
  • Higher-risk areas (active families held by Inogen, Philips/Respironics, CAIRE/Chart, Invacare, Belluscura and others, some filed in India via PCT): ultra-rapid-cycle compact POCs (e.g. US8894751, originally Inova Labs); breath-detection and dosing algorithms (Inogen "Intelligent Delivery Technology"); user-replaceable sieve cartridges (Inogen US 12,502,503, recent); specific rotary-valve, sound-damping and bed-geometry designs; product shapes covered by design patents (Inogen G3/G4 designs run to 2031+ in the US). Inogen has sued competitors over its patents before.
  • Jiayi's own IP: they hold Chinese utility-model and design patents (e.g. CN309605728S "S5" design, Nov 2025). Unless they filed in India, those don't apply here. I couldn't confirm any Indian or PCT filings by them; the patent database was unavailable when I searched. Copying their look exactly is still unwise: it invites passing-off claims if they enter India, and it weakens your own brand.
How to redesign safely
  1. Commission a freedom-to-operate (FTO) search by an Indian patent attorney (₹1.5–5 lakh). Search InPASS (Indian Patent Office) and Google Patents (country IN) under CPC codes B01D 53/047, A61M 16/10 and A61M 16/0677, by the applicants above.
  2. Base the gas system on documented expired PSA cycles, with your own bed sizing and cycle timing tuned for heat, dust and altitude.
  3. Write your own breath-trigger and dosing firmware (pressure threshold, timing windows, fixed-rate fallback) and avoid any specific claimed adaptive logic. Keep dated engineering records.
  4. Use your own industrial design (shape, controls, colours) and register the design and trademark in India before launch.
  5. File your own Indian patents on genuine improvements (e.g. power-cut and voltage-fluctuation handling, altitude compensation, dust-resistant intake).

6.4 Benefits of making your own

  • Lower cost: no ~13% non-reclaimable import duty and cess on finished units. You control the bill of materials, so you can price a portable at ₹50–70k with good margins.
  • Government and institutional demand: preference on GeM and in tenders for ≥50% local content (Army, ITBP, state health missions); "Made in India" credibility.
  • Schemes: Scheme for Strengthening the Medical Device Industry (₹500 crore, Nov 2024: components, testing, clusters; proposals were called in June–July 2026), medical device parks with shared testing labs, state capital subsidies. The ₹3,420 crore PLI scheme mainly targets imaging and implants, so it is unlikely to cover a concentrator.
  • Product fit for India: design for voltage swings, 45 °C summers, dust and 3,000–4,500 m altitude. Imported units handle these badly.
  • Owning the IP and brand: export later to South and Southeast Asia, the Middle East and Africa, and sell spares and service recurring revenue.
  • Supply security: COVID showed how importers lose out when China prioritises its own demand.
