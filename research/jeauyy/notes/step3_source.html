Launch strategy: a doctor-led respiratory home-care company for India

Step 3 of the study · 13 Nov 2026 · Facts are sourced (list at the end). Forecasts, market-size splits and unit estimates are my own modelling and are marked as estimates. Treat the financials as a planning model, not a promise.

The strategy in one page

The thesis. Don't build an oxygen-box importer. Hardware alone is a poor business: Inogen, the world leader in portables, sold 189,400 units for US$349 million in 2025 at a 44% gross margin and still made almost nothing (adjusted EBITDA US$2.7 million, net loss US$22.7 million). ResMed, which pairs devices with recurring consumables, cloud data and physician workflow, earns about 60% gross margin and 36% EBITDA and is valued near 8× revenue. Build the ResMed model for India's chronic respiratory patients, and use oxygen as the entry wedge.

  1. Position: "Prescribed, titrated, monitored." Oxygen sold through pulmonologists with a titration protocol and a usage report. Today nearly every portable in India is sold by a retailer with no clinical check.
  2. Model: double engine, sale-led. Sell to chronic patients (with EMI). Rent for travel, altitude, post-discharge and try-before-you-buy. Aim for about 70% sale / 30% rental revenue in year 1, with rental feeding sales.
  3. Price: portable at ₹84,900 doctor-programme price (MRP ₹99,900) against ₹1.25–2.1 lakh for the brands. Home 5 L at ₹34,900. Home + Away bundle at ₹1,09,900.
  4. First 6 months after launch: ₹2.0 crore revenue (conservative) to ₹5.3 crore (optimistic). Year 2: ₹9.7 crore to ₹30 crore. EBITDA turns positive at roughly ₹14–16 crore annual revenue.
  5. Scale: oxygen → sleep apnea (CPAP/BiPAP) → diagnostics and monitoring → Indian-designed devices → exports. Each step raises the exit multiple, from about 1–2× revenue for a distributor to 5–8× for a company with its own products and recurring revenue.
One timing conflict to resolve first. You want to launch in 4 months. Your own import licence (MD-15) typically takes 4–8 months. A 4-month launch is only realistic if you (a) import through a firm that already holds Indian licences, or (b) launch with stock that is already licensed in India while your own licence is processed. Decide this in week 1. The upside of the timing: a March 2027 launch lands just before the altitude season (Ladakh and Char Dham open from May).

The best seat: own the patient and the prescription. That means brand + doctor channel + service + data, then pull manufacturing in behind it. A pure importer can be undercut by the next container from China. A company the doctors trust, with patients on record, can't.

2. Industry outlook

  • India medical devices: about US$15–19 billion today; forecasts for 2030 range from US$30 billion to US$50 billion. Import dependence is 70–80%. Exports crossed US$4 billion in FY25. GST on most devices fell from 12% to 5% in September 2025.
  • India oxygen concentrators: US$197.5 million in 2024, forecast US$296.7 million by 2033 (4.8% a year). Stationary units are 53% of revenue; portables are the fastest-growing segment.
  • The adjacent prize is sleep apnea: an estimated 104 million working-age Indians have obstructive sleep apnea, 47 million of them moderate-to-severe. One research firm puts India's sleep-apnea device market at US$332 million (2024). It is mostly undiagnosed.
  • Deal evidence: KKR bought Healthium Medtech in 2024 for about US$840 million, roughly 8.5× revenue and 20× EBITDA. Cipla, India's respiratory drug leader, took 20% of iCaltech (a respiratory diagnostic device maker) for ₹5 crore in 2025: drug companies are buying into respiratory devices. Yuwell (China) bought into Inogen in January 2025.
  • Headwinds: price control risk (NPPA), cheap Chinese imports, low trust in after-sales service, and Oxymed's head start.

Oxymed is the one to study. It calls itself India's leading oxygen concentrator brand and the fastest-growing in CPAP. It has a full range (5 L from ₹31,999, 10 L, portable, CPAP, BiPAP), a co-founder-led team in Gurugram, and the widest dealer network. It is already walking the path I recommend, but through dealers and price, not through doctors and clinical evidence. That is your opening.

How much have they sold?

Nobody publishes India unit sales. What is known: Inogen sold 189,400 units worldwide in 2025, with US$139 million of its revenue from outside the USA. My estimate for true battery portables in India is 8,000–15,000 units a year (₹100–200 crore), low confidence. You can replace my estimate with facts in two weeks:

  • Buy import shipment data for HS code 90192090 (Volza, Seair, Zauba; roughly ₹10–25k). It shows each importer's name, model, quantity and declared value by month. This tells you exactly how many units Oxymed's and Inogen's importers bring in, and their landed cost.
  • Interview 10 dealers and rental-store owners. They know monthly volumes by brand, return rates and what customers complain about.
  • Look up company filings (Ministry of Corporate Affairs, via Tofler or Zauba Corp) for Oxymed's legal entity to see revenue and profit.

4. Your unique selling proposition

You can't out-brand Philips or out-distribute Oxymed in year 1. You can be the only one that is clinically run. Five promises, each one provable:

PromiseWhat it means in practiceWhy rivals can't copy it quickly
1. Titrated, not just soldEvery patient gets a walk-test titration (6-minute walk with pulse oximeter) and a written oxygen prescription: setting at rest, on exertion, in sleep. If pulse dosing doesn't hold their oxygen level, you say so and offer a different solution.They sell through dealers and websites; they have no clinicians in the loop.
2. Honest numbersPublish oxygen output in ml per minute, purity at 45 °C, and performance at Leh altitude, tested by a third-party lab. No "5 litre" claims.The whole category markets "litre-equivalents". Being the honest one wins doctors.
3. 48-hour swap3-year warranty; if it fails, a replacement reaches the patient in 48 hours in launch cities. Keep 5% of stock as swap units.Needs local stock and discipline, not technology.
4. Half the price, pay monthly₹84,900 against ₹1.25–2.1 lakh, with no-cost EMI and a rent-first option. Spare battery at ₹14,900 against Oxymed's ₹26,000.Global brands can't drop price without hurting other markets.
5. The doctor sees the usageA monthly one-page report to the treating doctor: hours used, settings, oxygen-level trend. Starts as a simple app plus Bluetooth pulse oximeter; later built into your own device.This is the data asset. It compounds.

5. Driving it scientifically

  • Before launch (8 weeks): validation study. Bench-test 10 units for purity and dose at each setting, at room temperature and 45 °C; then field-test at Leh (3,500 m) and a 5,000 m pass. Publish as a white paper. Cost about ₹8–12 lakh.
  • Months 1–6: titration study. About 60 COPD and lung-fibrosis patients, comparing walk-test oxygen levels on your portable against cylinder oxygen. Ethics-committee approved, registered on the Clinical Trials Registry of India, led by your board pulmonologist. Target an abstract at NAPCON (the national chest conference) and a paper in Lung India.
  • Ongoing: a long-term oxygen registry with patient consent. Indian research already shows poor compliance with home oxygen. Nobody has national real-world data. You would.
  • Founding clinicians programme: 30 pulmonologists as an advisory group and study sites. They get the prescription pad, the titration protocol, the usage reports and early data.
  • For patients: plain-language education in Hindi and regional languages on YouTube and WhatsApp: "Do I need oxygen?", "portable vs home unit", real patient stories. Oxygen Times already wins search traffic with comparison videos; match it with doctor-led content.
Compliance line you must not cross. India's Uniform Code for Marketing Practices in Medical Devices (2024) and medical council rules bar gifts, commissions and per-unit payments to doctors. Pay doctors only fair fees for real research or advisory work, under written agreements. Your board pulmonologist must disclose the interest to patients and should not be the one selling to their own patients. Also, nothing can be promoted before its licence is granted. A company built on doctor trust loses everything with one referral-fee scandal.

Recommendation: double engine, sale-led.
  • Sale is the main engine because your channel is doctors treating chronic patients.
  • Rental is the second engine and the funnel: "rent for 30 days; the rent comes off the price if you buy". It also absorbs demo units and returns, so no stock is wasted.
  • Own the rental fleet only in your 2–3 launch cities. Elsewhere, supply existing rental stores, who want a cheaper portable than a ₹2 lakh Philips.
  • For altitude, partner with operators in Leh, Manali and the Char Dham route instead of running your own counters in year 1.

How to read this.
  • Break-even is at about ₹14–16 crore a year. Below that, the team and clinical programme cost more than the gross profit. That is the price of building a company and not a trading desk.
  • Margins rise with mix, not price: more direct sales, more rental, more consumables. Dealer-heavy growth is fast but thin (24% vs 42%).
  • Cash needed to reach break-even: roughly ₹6–9 crore (losses, stock for 3 months, rental fleet, licensing).
  • Biggest swing factors: how many doctors actually prescribe each month, the device's real-world failure rate, and whether the unit is quiet enough for patients to accept.

Exit logic. A distributor sells for about 1–2× revenue. A company with its own products, recurring revenue and clinical data sells for 5–8× (Healthium: 8.5×). Likely buyers of a stake: respiratory drug companies wanting devices (Cipla, Lupin), global device makers wanting India (ResMed, Yuwell/Inogen, Fisher & Paykel, Drive DeVilbiss), and healthcare investors (KKR, Quadria, HealthQuad and similar). At ₹150 crore revenue with your own device, a ₹600–1,000 crore valuation is a reasonable ambition. Everything in phases 1–2 should be done with that buyer's diligence checklist in mind: clean licences, clean doctor relationships, real data.

WhereUseWhen
In the deviceA portable already senses every breath. That gives breathing rate, activity and sleep patterns for free. How to be part of it: the scarce asset in medical AI is not models, it is consented, labelled, longitudinal patient data tied to outcomes. Your registry is that asset. Get consent and data governance right from the first patient (India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act applies), and note that software making clinical claims is itself regulated as a medical device.

12. First 120 days

  1. Weeks 1–2: choose the licence route (existing importer vs own). Order 10 sample units of P25B, P37A and Y05B. Buy import data. Sign the board pulmonologist and a regulatory consultant.
  2. Weeks 3–8: bench and Leh validation; measure noise and failure rate. If the unit fails here, change supplier now (Yuwell, Longfian and others are alternatives). Recruit 30 founding clinicians. File licences. Build brand, website, WhatsApp flow.
  3. Weeks 9–14: first commercial stock; train a 3-person service bench; sign 5 hospitals and 5 rental stores; ethics approval for the titration study.
  4. Weeks 15–17: launch in 2–3 cities; altitude partners signed before May.
