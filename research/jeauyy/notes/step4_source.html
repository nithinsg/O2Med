What this round of research changes

  1. Jiayi is one of at least a dozen Chinese makers, and not the most proven. Ten Chinese companies now hold US FDA clearance for a portable concentrator. Jiayi holds none. Its advantage is price: about US$370–440 a unit against US$700–1,200 for the FDA-cleared ones.
  2. Your main Indian rival almost certainly sells a rebadged Chinese unit. The Oxymed P2 matches the Kingon P2 from Qingdao (same 1.98 kg, same 5 settings). The same machine sells in the USA as the "Rhythm P2" for US$3,295 and in Australia under Kingon's own name. This is my inference from matching specs, not a confirmed fact.
  3. Hardware is turning into a commodity. 23 of the 30 US clearances in this product category since 2022 went to Chinese firms. Prices will keep falling. This strengthens the step-3 advice: win on doctors, service and data, not on the box.
  4. Don't export a rebadged Chinese unit. Anyone can buy the same box. Exports make sense only once you have your own design. The best first export markets are then the neighbours (Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka), the Gulf and the Andean countries, not the USA.
  5. A naming warning. Your working name "O2Med" is too close to "Oxymed", the market leader in the same category. Expect customer confusion and a trademark objection. Pick a different name before spending on brand.

Is there an exact replica of the Jiayi units? I found no proven clone. Jiayi itself lists the P05A as "JYPT1" on Global Sources, and a UK shop (Broxtal) sells it under the Jeauyy name. But there are close spec-twins: Kingon and Longfian both ship a 1.98 kg, 5-setting unit, and CAIRE's FreeStyle Comfort has the same 1,050 ml/min maximum output as Jiayi's P25B. CAIRE (US-owned, part of Japan's Niterra) runs a facility in Chengdu, the same city as Jiayi. Chengdu looks like a talent and supplier cluster for portable concentrators. That is a hypothesis worth asking Jiayi about.

What this means for sourcing.
  • Jiayi's low price is what makes an ₹84,900 retail price possible. A Kingon at about US$800 lands near ₹80,000 and can't be sold at that price.
  • But "FDA-cleared hardware" is a powerful line with doctors, and Kingon, Longfian, Yuwell and Jumao can say it. Jiayi can't.
  • Benchmark three units side by side in your validation study: Jiayi P25B, Kingon P2 and Longfian JAY-1000P. Compare purity, dose, noise, heat and failure rate. Then choose on data, and keep a second supplier qualified so you are never hostage to one.
  • Ask each maker for exclusivity in India for 2–3 years against volume commitments. Without it, a rival can import the same box.

How the business works abroad. In the USA and Europe the manufacturer rarely sells to the patient. It sells to home-care providers (Lincare, Apria, AdaptHealth in the USA; Linde, Air Liquide's VitalAire in Europe; Teijin in Japan), who rent equipment to patients and bill insurers. US Medicare pays the same monthly amount whatever equipment is used, so providers usually hand out cheap tanks or home units, and patients who want a portable often pay cash. That cash market is what Inogen built with television advertising. India has no such provider layer and almost no insurance cover for home oxygen, so in India you must be the brand and the provider at once. That is harder, and it is also why a well-run player can own the patient relationship.

Three patterns across all of them
  • Profits follow recurrence. ResMed (masks, software) and Yuwell (many consumer products) are highly profitable. Inogen (one device) isn't.
  • Quality failures are fatal. Philips lost its US franchise through one defect and slow handling of complaints.
  • China now sets the hardware price. Western brands survive on brand, evidence, service life and channel control. An Indian company must plan to do the same.

My answer: India first is right. It is the one market where your doctor network counts, where the brands are overpriced and where local manufacturing is rewarded. If you had to pick one market outside India, pick the Gulf for portables (money, Indian diaspora, fast review) and donor-funded tenders for a rugged home unit. Use Nepal and Bhutan as a cheap rehearsal in year 2. Leave the USA until you have something the ten Chinese entrants don't.

Part E. How to found a medtech company, step by step

The frameworks below are the ones taught at Stanford (the Biodesign process: identify, invent, implement), MIT (Bill Aulet's "beachhead market"), and by Steve Blank (customer discovery) and Clayton Christensen ("jobs to be done"). I have applied them to your case.

Step 1. Write the problem statement before anything else

Stanford Biodesign's format is: a way to [solve a problem] in [a population] in order to [achieve an outcome]. A product is not a problem. "Sell portable concentrators" is not a problem statement. Here is a draft for you:

Draft problem statement. "A way to give Indian adults with chronic low-oxygen lung disease (COPD, post-TB lung damage, lung fibrosis) oxygen therapy that is correctly dosed, affordable and checked over time, at home and on the move, so that they use it the 15 or more hours a day that the evidence says is needed, stay active and stay out of hospital."

Why this wording matters: the survival benefit of long-term oxygen comes only with about 15+ hours of use a day. Indian research shows patients fall short. So the problem is adherence and correct dosing, and the device is one part of the answer. This is what separates you from a box-seller.

Step 2. Test the problem with real people (4–6 weeks)

  • Interview 30 patients and families, 15 pulmonologists, 10 dealers and rental owners, 5 hospital discharge nurses. Ask about the last time they bought or prescribed oxygen: what happened, what it cost, what went wrong.
  • Don't pitch. You are looking for the top three pains and for who actually decides and pays (often the patient's adult child).
  • Pick one beachhead: a narrow first market you can win outright. Mine for you: mobile COPD and fibrosis patients of your 30 pulmonologists, in 2–3 cities.

Step 4. Regulatory and quality: the part that makes it "medtech"

  • Licences as set out in step 2 of this study (sale registration MD-42, import licence MD-15, later manufacturing licence MD-5).
  • Start a simple quality system on day one, aligned with ISO 13485: supplier approval, incoming inspection, serial-number traceability, complaint log, adverse-event reporting, recall procedure. Even an importer needs this, and it is the first thing an acquirer audits.
  • Hire or contract one experienced regulatory and quality person early. This is not a job for an intern.

Step 6. Build the brand

  • Positioning sentence: "For people living with chronic lung disease and the doctors who treat them, [brand] is the respiratory care company that prescribes, fits and follows up, not just sells a machine."
  • Name: short, easy to say in Hindi and English, nothing like Oxymed, Oxysure, Inogen or ResMed, and free as a trademark and a .com/.in. Have a trademark attorney search before you fall in love with a name.
  • Proof before polish: the validation study, the 48-hour swap promise and real patient stories do more than a logo.
  • Two voices: evidence and data for doctors; warmth and plain language for patients and families.
  • Every touchpoint is the brand: the box, the manual in the patient's language, the WhatsApp reply time, the engineer's visit.

Step 8. The order of operations

  1. Problem interviews and beachhead choice (weeks 1–6).
  2. Company, trademark, founders' agreement, regulatory consultant (weeks 1–4, in parallel).
  3. Supplier benchmark: three Chinese units tested side by side (weeks 3–10).
  4. Licences filed; quality system started (weeks 4–12).
  5. Founding clinicians signed; titration protocol written (weeks 6–12).
  6. Brand, website, patient support flow (weeks 8–14).
  7. Launch in 2–3 cities; measure doctor repeat-prescription rate, device failure rate, patient hours of use (month 4 onward).
  8. Seed round on that evidence; add sleep apnea; begin own-device design (months 9–18).
The five mistakes that kill young medtech companies
  • Starting with a product and hunting for a problem.
  • Treating regulation and quality as paperwork to do later.
  • Paying doctors for prescriptions. It is barred in India and it poisons the brand.
  • Growing through dealers so fast that service collapses.
  • Depending on one supplier with no contract and no backup.
