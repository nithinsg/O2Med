A refresher for clinicians · oxygen, altitude and flight

Oxygen, from alveolus to Kedarnath

The physiology of oxygen and carbon dioxide, how home and portable concentrators work, who needs long-term oxygen, what a flight cabin does to a COPD patient, and what the evidence does and does not say about using oxygen to prevent altitude illness.

Start here

The questions that matter for the product

Will a portable concentrator prevent AMS?Not established

Guidelines recommend gradual ascent and acetazolamide for prevention. Oxygen is not among the recommended prophylactic measures. Small studies of oxygen-enriched rooms at night improved sleep, SpO₂ and next-day performance, but there is no trial showing that an ambulatory pulse-dose device prevents AMS.

Will it prevent HAPE or HACE?No evidence

Oxygen is a first-line treatment for both, alongside descent. Biologically it should help (it reverses hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, the driver of HAPE), but no study has tested intermittent portable oxygen as prevention. Descent remains the treatment that cannot be replaced.

Does it replace acclimatisation?No

Oxygen only works while it is flowing. Take the cannula off and SpO₂ falls back within minutes. Acclimatisation is driven by hypoxia itself, so continuous oxygen may even slow it. Ascent rules still apply.

Does it help at all?Yes, while in use

It raises inspired oxygen, which lowers the "physiological altitude" the body feels. That relieves hypoxaemic symptoms, can improve sleep and exercise tolerance, and buys time to descend. Its job is relief and safety, not prevention.

Who on a flight is at risk?Lung and heart patients

The cabin is pressurised to an altitude of up to 2,438 m (8,000 ft). Healthy passengers drop to SpO₂ around 90–93% and feel nothing. COPD, ILD, pulmonary hypertension and patients already on oxygen can fall well below 85%.

What can AirO₂ say?Relief, not prevention

"Supplies supplemental oxygen and raises blood-oxygen levels while in use" is supportable. "Prevents altitude sickness" or "replaces acclimatisation" is not, without your own trial. See the claims chapter.

01 · Physiology

The oxygen cascade

Oxygen moves down a pressure gradient from the air to the mitochondria. At each step some pressure is lost. Altitude lowers the very first step, so everything downstream falls with it.

PIO₂ = FIO₂ × (PB − 47) inspired, humidified at 37 °C PAO₂ = PIO₂ − PaCO₂ / R alveolar gas equation, R ≈ 0.8 CaO₂ = 1.34 × Hb × SaO₂ + 0.003 × PaO₂ ml O₂ per dl blood DO₂ = CO × CaO₂ × 10 ml O₂ delivered per minute

Two things to keep in mind. First, the fraction of oxygen in air stays 20.9% all the way up Everest: what falls is barometric pressure. Second, almost all the oxygen the tissues use is carried on haemoglobin. Dissolved oxygen (the 0.003 term) is tiny unless the patient is breathing high pressures, which is why SpO₂ and Hb matter more than PaO₂ for delivery.

Rough guide: each 1 L/min by nasal cannula adds about 3–4 percentage points, less if breathing fast or through the mouth.

Model: standard atmosphere, alveolar gas equation with altitude hyperventilation (PaCO₂ falls about 2.5 mmHg per 1,000 m in health, less with lung disease), Severinghaus dissociation curve corrected for the respiratory alkalosis (left shift). An estimate for teaching, not a measurement.

Reading the curve

  • The flat top (PaO₂ above about 60 mmHg, SaO₂ above 90%) is a safety margin. A healthy person at 2,000 m barely changes saturation.
  • The steep part starts below about 60 mmHg. Here a small fall in PaO₂ causes a large fall in saturation. Most of the Himalayan pilgrim sites put people on this slope, and a COPD patient may start there at sea level.
  • Shifts: alkalosis (hyperventilation at altitude), cold and low 2,3-DPG move the curve left, which helps loading in the lung. Acidosis, fever and raised 2,3-DPG move it right, which helps unloading in tissue. P50 in adults is about 27 mmHg.
02 · Physiology

Five reasons SpO₂ falls, and which ones oxygen fixes

Hypoxaemia means low oxygen in arterial blood. Hypoxia means too little oxygen at the tissue, which can also happen with normal blood gases (anaemia, low cardiac output, carbon monoxide, cyanide). The A–a gradient sorts the causes.

MechanismExampleA–a gradientResponse to oxygen
Low inspired O₂Altitude, aircraft cabinNormalExcellent: the cause is removed
HypoventilationOpioids, neuromuscular disease, obesity hypoventilationNormalCorrects SpO₂ but not CO₂; can mask a rising PaCO₂
V/Q mismatchCOPD, asthma, pneumonia, pulmonary embolismRaisedGood: the commonest cause in practice
Diffusion limitationILD, exercise at altitudeRaised, worse on exertionGood
ShuntConsolidation, ARDS, intracardiac right-to-leftRaisedPoor: blood bypassing ventilated alveoli never sees the extra oxygen

At altitude two mechanisms stack: low inspired oxygen for everyone, plus diffusion limitation during exertion because red cells spend less time in the capillary. In HAPE, edematous lung adds shunt and V/Q mismatch, which is why HAPE saturations are far lower than the altitude alone predicts.

03 · Physiology

Carbon dioxide, "washout" and the COPD retainer

Oxygen and carbon dioxide are handled differently. Oxygenation depends mostly on FIO₂ and V/Q matching. CO₂ removal depends almost entirely on alveolar ventilation:

PaCO₂ ≈ 0.863 × VCO₂ / VA halve alveolar ventilation, double PaCO₂

Giving oxygen does not remove CO₂. Only breathing does. "Washout" gets used for three different things, and it helps to keep them apart:

CO₂ washout

Ventilation clearing CO₂

Each breath dilutes alveolar CO₂. At altitude, hypoxia drives ventilation up, PaCO₂ falls (to around 30 mmHg at 3,500 m) and a respiratory alkalosis follows. That alkalosis is what acetazolamide partly corrects.

Dead-space washout

High-flow nasal oxygen

Flows of 30–60 L/min flush CO₂-rich gas out of the nose and pharynx, so the next breath has less rebreathed CO₂. Low-flow cannulas and pulse-dose portables do not do this.

Nitrogen washout

Pre-oxygenation

Breathing high FIO₂ before intubation replaces nitrogen in the lungs with oxygen, building a reservoir that delays desaturation during apnoea.

Why oxygen can raise CO₂ in some COPD patients

  • Loss of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction. Oxygen opens vessels to poorly ventilated units, so more blood goes to areas that do not clear CO₂. This is the main mechanism.
  • Haldane effect. Oxygenated haemoglobin holds less CO₂, releasing it into the plasma.
  • Reduced hypoxic drive. Contributes, but less than taught.
In patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure (COPD with prior hypercapnia, obesity hypoventilation, neuromuscular or chest-wall disease) target SpO₂ 88–92%. For most other acutely ill adults, 94–98% (BTS 2017).
04 · Physiology

Hyperoxaemia: when more oxygen is not better

Above about 97–98% saturation, haemoglobin is full, so extra oxygen only adds dissolved gas and raises PaO₂. That carries costs:

Mechanisms of harm

  • Reactive oxygen species and inflammation
  • Coronary and cerebral vasoconstriction
  • Absorption atelectasis at high FIO₂ (nitrogen washed out, alveoli collapse)
  • Hypercapnia in at-risk patients (chapter 3)
  • Pulmonary oxygen toxicity with prolonged FIO₂ above about 50–60%

What the trials showed

  • AVOID (2015): oxygen in non-hypoxaemic STEMI increased early myocardial injury.
  • DETO2X-AMI (2017): no mortality benefit from oxygen in suspected MI without hypoxaemia.
  • IOTA meta-analysis (2018): liberal oxygen increased mortality in acutely ill adults compared with conservative oxygen.

For a portable concentrator the practical point is different: a pulse-dose device delivering about 90% oxygen into a nasal cannula cannot produce dangerous hyperoxaemia in a person breathing at altitude. The real risk in a home setting is hypercapnia in a COPD retainer given more than prescribed, and fire.

05 · Equipment

How a concentrator makes oxygen

Every concentrator, the 18 kg home 5 L unit and the 1.5 kg Stride 2 alike, uses pressure swing adsorption (PSA). Room air is compressed into a column of zeolite (usually a lithium-exchanged LiX type). Under pressure, zeolite traps nitrogen and lets oxygen and argon through. Two columns take turns: one produces while the other is depressurised and vents its nitrogen back into the room.

The beds swap every few seconds. A small purge of product oxygen flows back through the resting bed to help release nitrogen. Final gas is about 93% oxygen, the rest mostly argon (which zeolite does not separate).

Continuous flow (home 5 L) vs pulse dose (portables)

Continuous

Home 5 L unit

Delivers a steady flow, 1–5 L/min, whether the patient is breathing in or out. About two-thirds of it is wasted during expiration, but it works during sleep, mouth breathing and with humidifiers or nebulisers. Purity falls above the rated flow.

Pulse

Portable concentrators

A pressure sensor detects the start of inspiration and fires a bolus (say 10–50 ml) into the cannula. Efficient, so a small compressor and battery can last hours. But "setting 3" is not "3 L/min": the oxygen per minute depends on the device and on breathing rate, and triggering can fail with shallow breaths, mouth breathing or during sleep.

The "4% per litre" rule is a teaching approximation. Real FIO₂ falls as minute ventilation rises, which is exactly what happens on exertion and at altitude.

Pulse devices at altitude. A bench study of four portable concentrators found that delivered oxygen per minute often fell as breathing rate rose and did not match the "litre equivalent" claimed (Chatburn and Williams, 2010). Concentrators also deliver a volume of gas at ambient pressure, so each millilitre contains fewer oxygen molecules higher up. Titrate to SpO₂ on the patient, at rest and walking, rather than to the setting number.
06 · Clinical

Who needs long-term oxygen therapy

Two trials from 1980–81 still define LTOT. In COPD with severe resting hypoxaemia, oxygen for at least 15 hours a day (MRC) and near-continuous oxygen (NOTT) reduced mortality. That benefit has never been shown for milder hypoxaemia.

GroupCriteria (stable, on optimal treatment)Evidence
Severe resting hypoxaemiaPaO₂ ≤ 55 mmHg (7.3 kPa) or SaO₂ ≤ 88%Survival benefit in COPD: NOTT 1980, MRC 1981. Prescribe ≥ 15 h/day.
With end-organ effectsPaO₂ 56–59 mmHg (7.3–8.0 kPa) or SaO₂ 89% plus cor pulmonale, peripheral oedema or haematocrit > 55%Same trials
Moderate desaturationResting SpO₂ 89–93%, or desaturation on exercise onlyNo benefit on survival or admissions: LOTT 2016
Nocturnal-only desaturationCOPD desaturating at night onlyNo benefit: INOX 2020
Exertional (ambulatory) oxygenSevere desaturation on walkingATS 2020: conditional recommendation in COPD and ILD. AmbOx 2018: better quality of life in ILD.

Typical LTOT patients: COPD (the majority), interstitial lung disease, pulmonary hypertension, cystic fibrosis, severe chronic heart failure with hypoxaemia, and some neuromuscular patients alongside ventilation. They need a home concentrator for sleep and most of the day. A portable is what lets them leave the house, which is where AirO₂'s portables fit: as ambulatory oxygen, on top of a home unit, not instead of it.

Smoking is the main safety exclusion. Oxygen-related burns and house fires happen mostly to patients who smoke while using oxygen.
07 · Clinical

What a flight does to a lung patient

Commercial cabins are pressurised to an equivalent altitude of up to 2,438 m (8,000 ft), PB about 565 mmHg. Inspired oxygen falls from about 150 to about 109 mmHg. In health, PaO₂ drops to around 60–70 mmHg and SpO₂ to about 90–93%, on the flat part of the curve. A patient who starts lower on the curve slides onto the steep part.

Most affected

COPD, ILD, pulmonary hypertension, cystic fibrosis, patients on LTOT, cyanotic congenital heart disease, severe heart failure, severe anaemia, sickle cell disease.

Other cabin risks

Gas expands about 30% at cabin altitude: a recent or unresolved pneumothorax, bullae and recent surgery need specific clearance. Long sitting raises VTE risk.

Assessment (BTS 2022)

Resting SpO₂ ≥ 95% on room air: usually no test needed. Lower values or significant disease: hypoxic challenge test (breathing 15% oxygen for 20 minutes). Desaturation below about 85% or PaO₂ below 6.6 kPa: in-flight oxygen. Patients on LTOT need in-flight oxygen, usually at an increased flow.

Practicalities for a portable: airlines accept only certain approved portable concentrators, the battery must fit lithium limits (under 100 Wh per battery without airline approval), and many airlines want enough battery for the whole journey plus a margin, often 150%. Each airline sets its own rules and paperwork.

08 · Clinical

Altitude illness: AMS, HACE and HAPE

Risk starts above about 2,500 m and depends far more on how fast people climb, especially sleeping altitude, than on how high they end up. Char Dham pilgrims who drive or fly to 3,000–3,600 m in a day, often older and with comorbidities, are a textbook moderate-to-high-risk group.

AMS

Acute mountain sickness

When: 6–12 h after arrival.
What: headache plus nausea, fatigue or dizziness (Lake Louise score 2018).
Why: not fully known; likely brain swelling from hypoxic vasodilation and changes in permeability.
Treat: stop ascending, rest, analgesia, antiemetic; acetazolamide 250 mg BD; oxygen; descend if worse.

HACE

High-altitude cerebral oedema

When: usually after AMS, often day 2–4.
What: ataxia, confusion, drowsiness. Ataxia (heel-to-toe walk) is the key sign.
Why: vasogenic oedema; the far end of the AMS spectrum.
Treat: descend now; dexamethasone 8 mg then 4 mg 6-hourly; oxygen; portable hyperbaric bag if descent is impossible.

HAPE

High-altitude pulmonary oedema

When: day 2–4, often at night.
What: breathless at rest, cough, falling exercise tolerance, very low SpO₂ for the altitude; may occur without AMS.
Why: exaggerated, uneven hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction → high capillary pressure → stress failure and leak.
Treat: descend; oxygen to SpO₂ > 90%; nifedipine 30 mg ER 12-hourly if no oxygen or descent.

Prevention, by the guidelines (WMS 2019, updated 2024)

09 · The product question

What extra oxygen does at altitude

Adding oxygen raises PIO₂, which is the same as moving to a lower altitude. West worked out that each 1% added to room oxygen concentration lowers the equivalent altitude by roughly 300 m. That gives a clear way to talk about what a device can do.

29% is roughly 2 L/min of continuous flow at rest. Pulse settings cannot be converted reliably; measure SpO₂ instead.

Why oxygen relieves but does not acclimatise

Acclimatisation is a response to hypoxia

  • Hours: carotid bodies raise ventilation (hypoxic ventilatory response).
  • Days: kidneys excrete bicarbonate to correct the alkalosis, letting ventilation rise further. Acetazolamide speeds this.
  • Days to weeks: plasma volume falls, haemoglobin concentration rises, then erythropoietin raises red cell mass.
  • Throughout: 2,3-DPG and capillary changes improve unloading.

What that means for an oxygen device

  • Every one of those steps needs a hypoxic signal. Oxygen removes the signal while it is on.
  • Used continuously, it could slow acclimatisation. Used for sleep, exertion or symptoms, the effect is not known.
  • The benefit stops within minutes of removing the cannula, so it cannot "pre-load" protection.
  • Using it to keep climbing with symptoms is dangerous: it can mask worsening AMS, HACE or HAPE.
A defensible clinical position: portable oxygen is a symptom-relief and safety tool for travellers with lung or heart disease and for anyone who develops altitude illness, used alongside gradual ascent and, where indicated, acetazolamide. It is not a substitute for either, and it does not change the rule that worsening illness means descent.
10 · Product

What AirO₂ can and cannot claim

Claims must match the device's registered intended use and the evidence behind it. For Stride 1, the registered use is to supply oxygen-enriched air for oxygen therapy or to relieve discomfort caused by hypoxia. Prevention claims need prevention data.

Supportable now

  • "Supplies supplemental oxygen, up to 93% concentration."
  • "Raises blood-oxygen levels while in use. Check with a pulse oximeter."
  • "Portable oxygen for travel, including at altitude, as advised by your doctor."
  • "Relieves symptoms of low oxygen" (matching the registered intended use).
  • Altitude rating, as tested: "Operates up to X m."

Not supportable without your own trial

  • "Prevents altitude sickness / AMS / HAPE / HACE."
  • "Replaces acclimatisation" or "no need to acclimatise."
  • "Climb safely without stopping."
  • "Equivalent to X L/min" for pulse settings.
  • Treating a named disease beyond the registered use.
The current website copy carries over Jiayi's line that Active mode is "for altitude sickness, palpitations and chest tightness", and "usable up to 6,000 m" for Stride 2. Both are the maker's claims. The first reads as a treatment claim and the second has not been verified on the device. Either substantiate them or soften them, for example "Active mode gives a steady 20 pulses a minute when breathing is too shallow to trigger the sensor."

A study you could actually run

Design: randomised, double-blind, sham-controlled trial of nocturnal portable oxygen in pilgrims arriving at Kedarnath by helicopter or road. Active arm: oxygen at a fixed setting during sleep. Sham arm: an identical device delivering room air.

Primary outcome: AMS (Lake Louise score ≥ 3 with headache) on the first morning. Secondary: overnight SpO₂, sleep quality, HAPE/HACE, descent or evacuation.

Size: if 40% of the sham arm develop AMS and oxygen cuts that to 25%, about 150 per arm gives 80% power at a 5% significance level.

Partners to approach: a high-altitude physiology group (for example DIPAS, DRDO's high-altitude institute), a teaching hospital near the Char Dham route, and an ethics committee registered with CTRI. The bench and chamber testing should come first; it also answers the 6,000 m question.
